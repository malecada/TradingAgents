Abstract

This study introduces the Multilayer Token Network (MLTN), a mathematical framework for analyzing Ethereum token transfers while capturing inter-token transformations crucial to Decentralized Finance (DeFi). Focusing on prominent fund accounts, we propose the PageRank-CheiRank Trade Balance (PCTB), an econometric measure inspired by balance sheet principles to quantify trade behavior over time. Applying MLTN to 2018–2024 transaction data, we reveal Alameda Research’s evolving trade strategies, fund interdependencies, and token-specific accumulation and distribution patterns, offering new insights into on-chain financial activities.

Introduction

Ethereum, introduced in 2015, is the leading blockchain platform. One of its main features is the presence of smart contracts—self-executing programs that operate on a decentralized network, specifically the Ethereum Virtual Machine (Buterin 2014). Using smart contracts, Ethereum enables tokenization, allowing the creation, destruction, and transfer of digital assets directly on the blockchain (Wang and Nixon 2021). This feature has contributed to the creation and expansion of Decentralized Finance (DeFi), a replication of traditional financial services in a decentralized environment (Schär 2021; Auer et al. 2024).

DeFi protocols facilitate a wide range of financial activities, including exchanging, lending, borrowing, and arbitrage, without the need for intermediaries. While this innovation offers users greater autonomy and flexibility, it also introduces new challenges. In a multi-token ecosystem, tokens interact dynamically across multiple markets, leading to complex interdependencies. Tokens can be traded within their own markets (intra-token activities) or transformed via mechanisms such as token swaps (inter-token activities). These interactions can amplify systemic risks, as evidenced by the collapse of FTX, a major cryptocurrency exchange, and its sister company, Alameda Research (Conlon et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2023). The downfall of FTX and Alameda Research in 2022 marked one of the most significant financial disruptions in the cryptocurrency industry. While extensive studies have analyzed FTX’s collapse, less attention has been given to Alameda Research’s trading activities despite its critical role in managing FTX customer funds. Understanding Alameda’s behavior requires a structured approach that takes into account the multi-token nature of blockchain transactions.

Analyzing financial activity on Ethereum is challenging due to the complex interplay between intra- and inter-token interactions, as well as the lack of established frameworks for systematically examining them. In our conference paper, we introduced a multi-token network representation for studying egocentric token transfer networks, shedding light on the activities of the Alameda Research network leading up to its bankruptcy in November 2022 (Coquide et al. 2024). Building upon this foundation, we extend multi-token network analysis by incorporating token transformation effects through a multilayer formalism. Our key contributions are as follows:

  • A multilayer network model captures both token-specific transactions and cross-token interactions.

  • A specialized PageRank and CheiRank measures that account for both intra-token and inter-token interactions.

  • Characterization of backbone ego networks centered on key fund addresses, incorporating network component analysis and community detection.

  • Temporal analysis of Alameda Research’s trading behavior, leveraging snapshot representations and a balance score based on PageRank and CheiRank centralities.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on Ethereum blockchain analysis and DeFi activities. Section 3 describes data preparation and filtering processes. Section 4 presents our multilayer network framework, backbone extraction methods, and centrality-based balance score computation. Section 5 discusses our findings, and Section 6 concludes the paper with insights and future research directions.

Related work

Early research explored the structural properties of Ethereum’s transaction networks, highlighting the role of graph-based methods in uncovering interactions between different entities (Chen et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2020). Temporal analyses further revealed the evolving dynamics of the Ethereum transaction network over time, including evidence of preferential attachment and the “rich-get-richer” phenomenon (Bai et al. 2021; Kondor et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2021).

Studies on the token networks within Ethereum have also been largely explored. Victor and Lüders analyzed ERC-20 token networks, examining their topology and interactions within the Ethereum ecosystem (Victor and Lüders 2019). Chen et al. provided a structural analysis of token interactions (Chen et al. 2020), while Morales et al. investigated user behavior and patterns of token adoption (Morales et al. 2023). Loporchio et al. characterized the topologies of ERC-20 and NFT token networks and found that these networks do not exhibit scale-free properties (Loporchio et al. 2023, 2024). Zhu et al. employed graph motifs to detect anomalies in the USDC token network during major market events, such as the LunaTerra collapse and Ethereum’s transition to Proof-of-Stake (Zhu et al. 2024).

The NFT trading network within Ethereum has drawn attention due to its unique features and rapid evolution. Casale-Brunet et al. conducted a graph analysis of the NFT network (Casale-Brunet et al. 2021), while Nadini et al. further mapped the NFT revolution by analyzing market trends, trade networks, and visual features (Nadini et al. 2021). Alizadeh et al. expanded this research, focusing on the structural characteristics and evolution of the NFT market (Alizadeh et al. 2023). Moreover, cycles within the token transfer network can be used to detect wash trading, a form of market manipulation in the NFT marketplace (La Morgia et al. 2023; Wachter et al. 2022).

In the context of decentralized finance (DeFi), Kitzler et al. analyzed DeFi contract call networks as nested structures, revealing systemic dependencies among protocols (Kitzler et al. 2023). Building on the prior work, Luo et al. investigated DeFi contract networks using graph embedding and clustering techniques to detect similarities across different DeFi services (Luo et al. 2024). Saengchote and Castro-Iragorri examined the evolving network topology of top DeFi actors and detected systemically important actors on a monthly basis throughout 2022 (Saengchote and Castro-Iragorri 2023). Additionally, Tovanich et al. introduced a motif-based approach to extract and classify DeFi functionalities, offering insights into the activities and behavioral patterns of DeFi actors (Tovanich et al. 2024).

Multilayer networks, introduced as a framework to model systems with multiple interdependent interactions (Boccaletti et al. 2014; Kivela et al. 2014), have gained increasing attention in financial research. In traditional finance, they have been applied to study systemic risks, interbank lending, and financial contagion (Poledna et al. 2015). These models have also been used to assess the systemic importance of financial institutions (Cao et al. 2021) and to examine information spillover effects in financial markets (Wang et al. 2021; Foglia et al. 2023). In the DeFi transaction network, Saengchote and Castro-Iragorri utilized multilayer network representations to analyze key actors (Saengchote and Castro-Iragorri 2023). Meanwhile, Ofori-Boateng et al. developed a topological approach for anomaly detection in dynamic blockchain transaction networks, demonstrating their potential for identifying illicit activities (Ofori-Boateng et al. 2021).

To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to apply the concept of multilayer networks to the analysis of token trading networks. While previous studies have examined individual token flows in isolation, they often neglect the interconnected nature of token exchanges within the DeFi ecosystem. By modeling token interactions as a Multilayer Token Network (MLTN), our approach extends prior work by capturing multi-token dependencies, enabling a deeper analysis of trading behaviors, interactions between participants, tokens, and protocols, as well as value flow dynamics in DeFi.

Data

In this section, we describe the dataset used for our analysis, detailing the structure of Ethereum transaction data, the process of collecting token transfer data, and the methods employed to filter out spam tokens and categorize addresses.

Background

The Ethereum blockchain serves as a foundation for Decentralized Finance (DeFi), enabling token transfers across a large and complex network of financial transactions. Ethereum addresses are classified into two types: Externally Owned Accounts (EOAs) and Contract Accounts (CAs). EOAs, controlled by private keys, typically belong to individual users or organizations, while CAs are linked to smart contracts that autonomously execute predefined functions and protocols.

Ethereum addresses are derived from the last 20 bytes of the Keccak-256 hash of an ECDSA public key. These addresses are pseudonymous, meaning that ownership is not directly tied to a real-world identity unless voluntarily disclosed or inferred through external means.

Ethereum supports various tokens that represent units of value. These tokens fall into two main categories:

  • Fungible Tokens Interchangeable tokens where each unit is identical and can be exchanged on a one-to-one basis. The most common standard for fungible tokens is ERC-20, which facilitates seamless interoperability across decentralized applications.

  • Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs) Unlike fungible tokens, NFTs are unique and indivisible. Each NFT represents a distinct token, such as digital art, collectibles, or virtual real estate. The ERC-721 standards govern the creation and management of NFTs on Ethereum.

In addition to these, the ERC-1155 standard introduces a multi-token framework, enabling a smart contract to manage multiple token types, including fungible, non-fungible, and semi-fungible tokens. This flexibility makes ERC-1155 particularly useful for applications such as gaming, digital collectibles, and tokenized assets that require hybrid token functionalities.

Token transfer data

To construct our network model, we first identified a set of seeding addresses to serve as focal points for data collection. In this work, we selected addresses labeled as “fund” addresses on Etherscan,Footnote 1 a well-known Ethereum block explorer platform, as these entities are actively engaged in on-chain token trading. As of December 2024, this Etherscan-labeled dataset consists of 90 Ethereum addresses. Notably, our dataset includes 27 addresses linked to Alameda Research, a cryptocurrency trading firm that played a central role in the FTX collapse, culminating in bankruptcy proceedings in November 2022.

For each address, we collected ego-token transfers from Alchemy, a blockchain infrastructure and API provider. Its Transfer API provides complete token transfer records, including ETH (both internal and external), ERC-20, ERC-721 (NFT), and ERC-1155 from and to a particular address.Footnote 2 Each recorded transfer includes the following information:

  • Block Number The identifier of the block in which the transaction is recorded.

  • Timestamp The timestamp when the transaction was confirmed on the blockchain.

  • Transaction Hash A unique identifier for the transaction.

  • From The address initiating the transfer (i.e., the sender’s address).

  • To The destination address to which the transfer is sent (i.e., the recipient’s address).

  • Token Type The type of token being transferred: ETH, ERC-20, ERC-721, or ERC-1155.

  • Token Value The quantity of tokens transferred in the transaction, expressed in the token’s native unit.

Our dataset comprises 2,754,707 token transfer records across 2,238,072 transactions, covering the period from block 4,892,604 (January 11, 2018, 21:03:55) to block 21,531,628 (December 31, 2024, 23:59:23).

Filtering out spam tokens

A major challenge in working with token transfer datasets is the presence of spam tokens that are distributed unsolicited to well-known addresses, creating the illusion of legitimacy. These tokens often bear misleading names to mimic reputable assets, potentially contaminating the dataset and distorting network analysis.

To address this issue, we used the Etherscan token reputation system to assess the credibility of each token in our dataset.Footnote 3 Etherscan classifies the tokens into six categories: UNKNOWN, NEUTRAL, OK, SUSPICIOUS, UNSAFE, and SPAM. Of the 12,755 distinct tokens in our dataset, we retained only transactions involving tokens labeled as “NEUTRAL” (1,137 tokens) or “OK” (97 tokens) reputations for our analysis. Other transactions involving any of the following reputations—“UNKNOWN” (5,223 tokens), “SUSPICIOUS” (3,016 tokens), “UNSAFE” (62 tokens), and “SPAM” (3,220 tokens)—are removed.

This filtering eliminated 117,286 transactions containing spam tokens, leaving 2,508,702 transfers across 2,120,786 transactions.

Labeled addresses

To enhance our analysis, we integrated publicly tagged addresses from Etherscan, which identifies prominent addresses and DeFi protocols by their hash addresses.Footnote 4 For each fund account, we performed web scraping on pages listing transactions and ERC-20 token transfers to compile a list of labeled addresses. We further expanded our labeled addresses by incorporating two publicly available GitHub repositories containing Etherscan-labeled addresses: (1) dawsbot/eth-labelsFootnote 5 (last updated: July 24, 2024) and (2) brianleect/etherscan-labelsFootnote 6 (last updated: October 1, 2023). Only addresses not already present in our labeled addresses were included from these sources.

A single user or DeFi protocol may control multiple addresses on the blockchain. For example, Alameda Research, the focus of our study, controls 27 distinct addresses (as tagged by Etherscan), each associated with different activities. Similarly, DeFi protocols like Compound or Uniswap operate through multiple contracts, each corresponding to a different liquidity pool.

To systematically group addresses belonging to the same entity—whether an individual, an organization, or a DeFi protocol—we use the colon (:) as a separator, treating the prefix as the entity name. For example, Etherscan tags such as “Compound: cETH Token” and “Compound: cDAI Token” are both assigned to the “Compound”, a prominent lending-borrowing protocol, while “Uniswap V2: Router 2” and “Uniswap V2: USDC-GRT” belong to “Uniswap V2”, one of the leading decentralized exchange protocols.

To further standardize entity names, we remove numerical suffixes at the end of labels, converting names like “Binance 14” to “Binance” and “Coinbase 10” to “Coinbase”, both of which are addresses belonging to centralized exchange companies. However, we preserve distinctions between protocol versions, such as “Uniswap V2” and “Uniswap V3”, since they refer to different versions of the protocol. Addresses without labels are treated as individual entities.

Using this approach, we grouped addresses that belong to the same entity, identifying 727 distinct entities across 1,961 addresses (1.70% of the total addresses). Despite representing a small proportion of the total addresses, these tagged entities are responsible for 1,348,846 out of 2,508,702 token transfers (53.78%).

Methodology

This section outlines the methodology used to construct and analyze a multilayer network model derived from Ethereum blockchain data. We describe our approach for extracting the network’s backbone to identify significant interactions and present the procedure for calculating centralities, including the well-known PageRank centrality and its complementary centrality, named the CheiRank (Zhirov et al. 2010). These centrality measures enable us to assess the economic importance and influence of Ethereum actors within the network.

Multi-token network

We begin by revisiting the multi-token network (MTN) introduced in our previous work (Coquide et al. 2024). The MTN represents token transfers between addresses as a pseudo-monolayer, consisting of N nodes being pairs of (address, token). Here, \(N\le N_{a}\times N_{t}\), where \(N_{a}\) is the number of addresses and \(N_{t}\) is the number of distinct tokens. Each node is denoted as \(i=(a,\alpha )\), where \(a\) represents the address and \(\alpha\) is the token.

The adjacency matrix \({A}\) representing the flow in token \({\alpha}\) from address \({a}\) to address \({a'}\), associated with nodes \(i= (a,\alpha)\), and \(i'= (a',\alpha)\) respectively, is as follows: \(\begin{array}{l}A_{i'i} = M^{a'}_{a}(\alpha)\label{eq:adjmtn} \end{array}\)where \(M_{a}^{a'}(\alpha)\) is the number of distinct transfers of token \({\alpha}\) transfer from address \({a}\) to address \({a'}\). This model does not account for interactions between nodes associated with different tokens. Consequently, it can be viewed either as a multilayer network without interactions between layers or as a mono-layer network where layer information is encoded in the node labels.

Multilayer token network

We propose a multilayer network model (MLTN) that represents token transfers between a set of ego addresses (egos) and their associated alters (alters). Such a network, denoted as \(\mathcal {M}\), consists of a set of nodes \(\mathcal {V}\) corresponding to the addresses, where \(|\mathcal {V}| = N_{a}\) is the total number of addresses and a set of layers \(\mathcal {L}\), each representing a distinct token, where \(|\mathcal {L}|=N_{t}\) is the number of involved tokens. The network also includes a set of directed edges \(\mathcal {E}\) that represent token flows between pairs of address-token nodes. These edges can be categorized into two types: inter-layer edges, which represent interactions between different tokens, and intra-layer edges, which represent interactions within the same token. The major difference with MTN approach described in Coquide et al. (2024) resides in the integration of inter-layer edges. Due to these changes, we choose to consider a tensor formalism, as exposed in De Dominico et al. (2015).

The adjacency tensor describes the directed token flow from a pair of address-token nodes \(i = (a,\alpha )\) to a destination \(i' = (a',\alpha')\), with \({i,i'} \in \Omega\) the super-set of address-token pairs, is defined as:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {A}^{i'}_{i} = M^{a'}_{a}(\alpha,\alpha'), \end{aligned}$$
(1)

where \(M^{a'}_{a}(\alpha,\alpha')\) represents the number of transactions in which token \(\alpha\) is transferred from address \(a\), to token \(\alpha'\) at address \(a'\). It is important to note that for inter-layer edges, where the source and destination tokens differ, the source and destination addresses must be identical. Any address \(a \in \mathcal {V}\) that is not involved in any transfers associated with layer \(\alpha\) is considered an isolated node, called a virtual node. We define \(N=|\Omega|=N_{a}N_{t}\) as the total number of MLTN nodes and \(N'=N-N_{v}\) as the number of non-virtual nodes, where \(N_{v}\) is the number of virtual nodes.

To establish connections between two layers and the same address \(a\) in the multilayer network, we propose a heuristic for creating interlayer edges (teleportation) based on the following rule: Within a single transaction, a directed edge is added from the source node (\(a, \alpha\)) in token layer \(\alpha\) to the destination node (\(a\),\(\alpha'\)) in token layer \(\alpha'\) whenever out-token \(\alpha\) and in-token \(\alpha'\) are associated with the same address. This in-out relationship indicates an exchange occurring within the same transaction.

Backbone network

The backbone of a network represents a subgraph that captures the most significant interactions between entities. Given the directed nature and token multiplicity in the multilayer token network (MTLN), we apply the multi-scale backbone extraction method from Güell et al. (2017), which considers both edge weights and directions when determining the significance of edges.

Given two node indices \(i=(a,\alpha )\) and \(i'=(a',\alpha )\), where \(k^{in}_{i}\) (resp. \(k^{out}_{i}\)) denotes the number of distinct partners sending (receiving) token \(\alpha\) to (from) address \(a\), the significance score associated with the total number of transfers from i to \(i'\) is computed as:

$$\begin{aligned} S_{i'i} = \mathcal {A}^{i'}_{i}/\sum _{k}\mathcal {A}^{k}_{i}, \end{aligned}$$
(2)

where \(\sum _{k}\mathcal {A}^{k}_{i}\), with \(k\in \Omega (\alpha )\), is the total number of transfers within layer \(\alpha\) with \(a\) being the sender. An edge is retained if its significance score exceeds an arbitrary threshold \(\kappa \in [0,1]\), which is determined by the condition:

$$\begin{aligned} (1-S_{i'i})^{k^{out}_{i}-1} < \kappa. \end{aligned}$$
(3)

Similarly, we define the incoming link significance score as:

$$\begin{aligned} S_{ii'} = \mathcal {A}^{i}_{i'}/\sum _{k}A^{i}_{k}, \end{aligned}$$
(4)

and the condition to keep it in the backbone is

$$\begin{aligned} (1-S_{ii'})^{k^{in}_{i}-1} < \kappa \end{aligned}$$
(5)

The significance threshold \(\kappa\) has been chosen so that the final backbone contains at most \(3\%\) of the total number of nodes for a sake of visualization, and at least \(50\%\) of the cumulated edge weights. A set of \(\kappa\) from 1 to 0.001 with \(log_{10}\) subdivisions was tested and a \(\kappa = 0.001\) is retained. The extracted backbone contains \(\approx 3\%\) of the total nodes with a cumulative weight \(\approx 55\%\). The choice of the threshold remains somewhat arbitrary, as is common in backbone extraction methods. An alternative is the Statistically Validated Network (SVN) approach proposed by (Tumminello et al. (2011), which uses a synchronous activity null model to identify significant links (Baltakys et al., 2018) extends this model for multiplex networks, and (Musciotto et al., 2021) could offer deeperinsights considering not only significant interactions but significant absences of trade.

Centrality

Following the mathematical framework from De Dominico et al. (2015), we describe our approach to calculating centrality measures, including PageRank and CheiRank, within the multilayer token network (MLTN).

Random walk and actor economic importance

We utilize PageRank centrality to assess the economic importance of Ethereum addresses. In a monolayer network, PageRank corresponds to the leading eigenvector of the Google matrix, which describes a stochastic process on a directed graph. For the MTLN, where node interactions are represented by a 2-Rank tensor \(\mathcal {A}^{i'}_{i}\), PageRank is the leading eigentensor of the Google tensor \(\mathcal {G}\), defined as:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {G} = \eta \mathcal {S} + (\eta - 1) \mathcal {T}, \end{aligned}$$
(6)

where \(\eta \in [0,1]\) is the damping factor, \(\mathcal {S}\) is the stochastic tensor representing the Markov process associated with the random walk through the multilayer network, and \(\mathcal {T}\) is a teleportation tensor which is either uniform \(\mathcal {T}^{i'}_{i} = 1/N\), \(\forall i' \in \Omega\), or can be tuned in case of a preferential PageRank. The stochastic tensor \(\mathcal {S}\) is obtained from \(\mathcal {A}\) following:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {S}^{i'}_{i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \frac{\mathcal {A}^{i'}_{i}}{\sum _{j\in \Omega }\mathcal {A}^{j}_{i}} & \text{ if } \sum _{j\in \Omega }\mathcal {A}^{j}_{i}> 0\\ \frac{1}{N} & \text{ otherwise, } \end{array} \right. \end{aligned}$$
(7)

where \(\sum _{j}\mathcal {A}^{j}_{i} = 0\) is associated to an address \(a\) being a dangling node in layer \(\alpha\). The CheiRank as described in Zhirov et al. (2010) is obtained by applying the PageRank metric to the edge-reversed MLTN. The associated adjacency tensor is \(\mathcal {A}^{*}\) such as \(\mathcal {A}^{*i'}_{i} = \mathcal {A}^{i}_{i'}\).

Both the method for handling dangling nodes and the teleportation tensor terms can be personalized in the PageRank centrality algorithm. In this study, we propose three variants of PageRank to capture the impact of inter-layer edges, as well as a baseline model based on a MLTN without inter-layer edges (i.e., MTN):

  1. 1.

    Baseline Uniform PageRank model (M0 PR) This centrality is measured on the MLTN by removing all inter-layer edges, which is formally similar to performing a uniform PageRank on the MTN. This model does not capture inter-layer dynamics and serves as a baseline to measure other models efficiency in capturing those effects.

  2. 2.

    Uniform PageRank model (Standard PR) This is a uniform PageRank model applied to the full MLTN.

  3. 3.

    Personalization PageRank model (Biased PR) The teleportation tensor \(\mathcal {T}\) is biased toward inter-layer jumps, allowing teleportation between nodes associated with the same address \(a\), but different token layers \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha'\).

  4. 4.

    Two-step PageRank Model (2Step PR) This model balances token contributions by first calculating a standard PageRank with a preferential teleportation vector based on import volume \(V^{\alpha }_{a}\), then computing the PageRank with using token-specific PageRank values derived from this first iteration as preferential teleportation probabilities.

 M0 PR This baseline model is based on the MTN; there are no inter-layer edges, therefore the adjacency tensor terms \(\mathcal {A}^{a'\alpha'}_{a\alpha } = 0\) if \(\alpha \ne \alpha'\). Teleportations from dangling nodes and due to the teleportation tensor are uniform. It implies that when a random walker reaches a dangling node, it teleports uniformly to any other node across all layers.

Standard PR In this model, the stochastic tensor is built with uniform teleportation when the walker reaches a dangling node (Equation 7), and the teleportation tensor is uniform \(\mathcal {T}^{i'}_{i} = 1/N\), \(\forall i,i' \in \Omega\). Therefore, when a random walker reaches a dangling node, it teleports uniformly to any other node across all layers.

Biased PR For the Biased PR model, the stochastic tensor remains unchanged; the only difference from the standard PR is in the teleportation tensor \(\mathcal {T}\). In this case, the teleportation tensor \(\mathcal {T}\) is modified such that the walker prefers to teleport between inter-layer nodes with the same address \(a\). The teleportation probability of jumping from node \(i=(a,\alpha )\) to \(i'=(a,\alpha')\) is:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {T}^{i'}_{i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \frac{\mathcal {A}^{i'}_{i}}{\sum _{j\in \Omega }\mathcal {A}^{j}_{i}} & \text{ if } \sum _{j\in \Omega }\mathcal {A}^{j}_{i}> 0\\ \frac{1}{N_{t}} & \text{ otherwise, } \end{array} \right. \end{aligned}$$
(8)

where \(N_{t}\) is the total number of layers (tokens). This biases teleportation towards inter-layer edges, i.e., edges issued from inter-layer interactions will be preferentially used for teleportation. In case \(i\) has no outgoing inter-layer connections (\(\sum _{j}\mathcal {A}^{j}_{i} = 0\)), the teleportation is uniformly directed toward any layer \(\alpha'\).

2Step PR This model, inspired by the two-step matrix used by Google to analyze the world trade network, as described in Ermann and Shepelyansky (2015), ensures the balance between addresses and the proportionality of tokens to their trade volume. This approach allows to consider all addresses in a balanced way, independent of their richness, with traded tokens contribution proportional to their usage in the whole network.

The model is built in two steps: First, a PageRank is computed considering a standard PageRank model with a preferential teleportation vector \(v\) such that its value for a node \(i=(a,\alpha )\) is:

$$\begin{aligned} v_{i} = \frac{V^{\alpha }_{a}}{N_{a}\sum _{\alpha'}V^{\alpha'}_{a}}, \end{aligned}$$
(9)

where \(V^{\alpha }_{a}\) is the total weight incoming edges of the node \(a\) in the layer \(\alpha\) (import volume). In the case of a node \(a\) without any incoming links (only outgoing), the value is set to \(\frac{1}{N_{a}N_{t}}\). For CheiRank, the first preferential vector is associated with the export volume \(V^{*}\)

$$\begin{aligned} v^{*}_{i} = \frac{V^{*\alpha }_{a}}{N_{a}\sum _{\alpha'}V^{*\alpha'}_{a}} \end{aligned}$$
(10)

The second step computes PageRank using Equation 6, where the teleportation values are token-dependent and the same for all addresses. These values are derived from the PageRank scores obtained in the first iteration, reduced to the token space:

$$\begin{aligned} p_{\alpha } = \sum _a p_{a\alpha }. \end{aligned}$$
(11)

In the case of CheiRank, the PageRank value \(p\) are replaced by \(p^{*}\).

Finally, the teleportation tensor is given by:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {T}^{i}_{i'} = \frac{p_{i}}{N_{a}}, \forall i \in \Omega, \end{aligned}$$
(12)

where \(p_i\) is the PageRank score for node \(i\) and \(N_{a}\) is the total number of addresses.

For all considered models we use \(\eta = 0.85\) being a standard value used in the literature.

Projection of the centrality scores

Due to the fine-grained scale based on addresses and token representation, we measure centrality scores for different scales that are: address, group, token, and group-token. However, the presence of virtual nodes might impact the final centrality scores for the different considered projection scales. Therefore, we decide to exclude their contribution, such that the final score for the pair (\(a,\alpha\)) (\(a, t\)) is computed as:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{p}_{i'}=\frac{\mathbf{P}_{i'}}{\sum _{i\in \Omega'}\mathbf{P}_{i}}, \end{aligned}$$
(13)

with \(\Omega'\) being the super-set of nodes excluding virtual nodes, \(\mathbf{P}\) is the PageRank tensor, \(i'\) representing a pair \((a,\alpha )\) such that \(a\) is involved in \(\alpha\) in the dataset. Note that in the case of the CheiRank, the notation is \(\mathbf{P}^{*}\) and \({\mathbf{p}}^{*}\) for its renormalized version.

For address-level centrality, the projected score is:

$$\begin{aligned} p_{a}=\sum _{i\in \mathcal {V}'(a)}\mathbf{p}_{i}, \end{aligned}$$
(14)

where \(\mathcal {V}'(a)\) is the set of non-virtual nodes associated to the address \(a\). Note that in the case of centrality at the level of address-token, a bold notation is used; when considering a projection, we will use a non-bold notation. The projection onto groups of addresses \(p_g\) is obtained from \(p_a\) by summing the PageRank probabilities associated with all addresses \(a\) that belong to the group.

The PageRank centrality for an address group-token pair \((g,\alpha )\) is:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{p}_{g\alpha} = \sum _{a\in \mathcal {V}'(g)} \mathbf{p}_{a\alpha}, \end{aligned}$$
(15)

where \(\mathcal {V}'(g)\) is the set of addresses \(a\) belonging to the group \(g\).

The centrality score for a given token \(\alpha\) is:

$$\begin{aligned} p_{\alpha } = \sum _{g}\mathbf{p}_{g\alpha }. \end{aligned}$$
(16)

PageRank-CheiRank trade balance

The PageRank-CheiRank trade balance (PCTB) is an analog score to the well-known import–export balance (Coquide et al. 2020). Since the direction of edges in MTLN represents the token flow direction, PageRank and CheiRank probabilities are assumed to measure the token accumulating and dispersing capacity of Ethereum’s addresses. In the context of address scale, the balance score of an address noted \(B_{a}\) is

$$\begin{aligned} B_{a} = \frac{p^{*}_{a}-p_{a}}{p^{*}_{a}+p_{a}}. \end{aligned}$$
(17)

The balance score is in the \([-1,1]\) range. A negative (positive) value of the balance for an address \({a}\) indicated that tends to accumulate (disperse) tokens through the MTLN. Therefore, we can utilize this measure as a metric for trade behavior. We also define group-scale balance score as:

$$\begin{aligned} B_{g} = \frac{p^{*}_{g}-p_{g}}{p{*}_{g}+p_{g}}. \end{aligned}$$
(18)

Finally, within a group \(g\), we can measure the token-specific balance scores as:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{B}_{g\alpha } = \frac{\mathbf{p}^{*}_{g\alpha }-\mathbf{p}_{g\alpha }}{\mathbf{p}^{*}_{g\alpha }+\mathbf{p}_{g\alpha }}. \end{aligned}$$
(19)

This measure permits the inference of entities’ trade behavior at the token scale.

Results

We present and discuss the results in four parts. The first part presents descriptive statitics for the aggregated multilayer token network (MLTN), which covers the entire dataset period. The second part presents a network analysis of the backbone network and community detection. The third part compares different PageRank models on both the address group and token scales. The final part pertains to the temporal analysis of Alameda Research’s trade behavior, considering monthly-based snapshots.

Network descriptions

Table 1 summarizes the MLTN’s key network statistics, comparing them with those of other network representations and its backbone. The network consists of 149,195 active address-token pairs (non-virtual nodes) across 1,217 token layers, with a low connection density (\(\approx 7.10^{-6}\)). Inter-layer edges are minimal, making up only about 3% of total edges.

Table 2 details the number of weakly and strongly connected components for each network representation. Focus on the largest strongly connected components; the Multi-Token Network (MTN) (similar to the MLTN without inter-layer edges) forms a strongly connected subgraph limited to the ETH token layer. In contrast, including inter-layer links expands connectivity across 287 token layers. Although more compact, the largest SCC in the MLTN’s backbone retains approximately 5% of the nodes and 16% of the edges from the full backbone network, spanning 91 tokens.

Fig. 1 shows the degree distribution, considering both the full network and its largest strongly connected component (SCC). The MLTN exhibits a strong balance between in-degree and out-degree distributions, though this symmetry weakens for high-degree nodes (\(k> 50\)) in the full network. In contrast, the SCC maintains this symmetry. Removing inter-layer edges primarily affects the largest SCC, reducing the fraction of low-degree nodes (\(k \in [2,10]\)).

The SCC diameter distribution, with and without inter-layer edges, is presented in Fig. 2. Most SCCs (90%) have diameters \(d \le 3\), indicating that they frequently engage in dyadic interactions. SCCs linked to Alameda Research span a wider range, with all having \(d \ge 6\). Removing inter-layer edges increases the prevalence of higher-diameter SCCs, suggesting a change in local topology. In the full MLTN, the largest SCC spans 287 tokens, including the most widely traded ones, such as WETH, USDT, and ETH (Fig. 3). While inter-layer edges are few, they have a significant influence on the network structure.

Fig. 3 (a) presents the distribution of token usage across network models for both the full network and the largest strongly connected component (SCC). In all cases, the top 10 tokens, in terms of the number of transfers per token, account for over 50% of total transfers, while a large number of tokens contribute only marginally. Fig. 3 (b) and (c) further compare the percentage share of total transfers among the top 10 tokens. The most dominant tokens include major cryptocurrencies (ETH, WETH, and WBTC), as well as stablecoins (USDC and USDT). Notably, WETH is overrepresented in the largest SCC, likely due to its role as the ERC-20 wrapped version of ETH, which is commonly used in DeFi transactions.

Backbone network

Following the procedure outlined in subsection 4.3, we extracted the backbone network and projected it across all layers into the address network for further analysis. Table 1 presents the statistical properties of the backbone network in comparison to other network representations. Table 2 reports the number of connected components and the size of the largest strongly connected component. To analyze community structures within the backbone network, we employed two community detection algorithms: the stochastic block model (SBM) and the Louvain method (Blondel et al. 2008). SBM is a probabilistic model that infers latent group structures by optimizing the likelihood of observed connections, making it particularly useful for uncovering role-based or functional clusters within networks. The Louvain method, on the other hand, is a modularity-based algorithm that detects communities by iteratively optimizing modularity scores, ensuring that tightly connected nodes are grouped together. Accounts within the backbone network were categorized based on their roles and functionalities. Fig. 4 illustrates the distribution of addresses across different categories, with panel (a) showing the number of addresses per category and panels (b) and (c) displaying the percentage distribution of accounts for each SBM and Louvain cluster, respectively.

The figure indicates that SBM communities tend to organize nodes by account category. For example, Alameda Research accounts are primarily grouped into clusters 906 and 918, while other fund accounts are mostly located in cluster 977. DEX-related accounts are concentrated in cluster 456, whereas centralized exchanges predominantly fall into cluster 69. Unlabeled accounts are primarily distributed across clusters 670 and 871, with a notable distinction: accounts in cluster 871 primarily exhibit outgoing transfers (i.e., spending tokens to fund accounts), whereas accounts in cluster 670 predominantly receive funds from fund accounts, as indicated by their high proportion of incoming transfers. In contrast, the Louvain method groups nodes based on their connectivity and high modularity (0.7383), resulting in communities where key accounts, such as Alameda and other funding entities, serve as central hubs, interacting with various other accounts. Notably, in clusters 5 and 6, egos primarily engage with centralized exchanges, whereas clusters 9 and 18 conduct trades using decentralized exchanges (DEX) platforms. In larger clusters with a higher number of accounts (e.g., 4, 8, 15, and 17), fund accounts serve as hubs, facilitating the transfer of tokens between ego entities and unlabeled addresses, likely representing individual users and organizations.

Based on these results, we selected SBM for further analysis, as it more effectively differentiates accounts according to their functionalities and roles. Fig. 5 presents a node-link diagram of the backbone address network, revealing several key structural patterns:

  • The right side of the diagram captures token transfer activities primarily associated with Alameda Research-related accounts, while the left side reflects transactions involving other fund accounts.

  • The outer ring primarily consists of ego accounts interacting with unknown accounts (clusters 69 and 670), which are suspected to represent individual customers or organizations, forming a star-like pattern. Notably, Alameda 14 and 27 interact with accounts from clusters 670 and 871. Differences between light green (cluster 69), purple (cluster 690), and blue (cluster 871) clusters highlight distinct transactional behaviors.

  • Moving inward, we observe a densely connected cluster on the left-hand side, signifying its central role in network transactions. Fund accounts in cluster 977, such as those of Jump Trading, facilitate interactions with centralized exchanges in cluster 69, including Binance, Coinbase, and OKX.

  • The right-hand side contains distinct orange and red groups representing specific account categories. The red cluster, which includes Fund: 0×979...873, predominantly interacts with mining accounts in cluster 728. Cluster 456 (brown) features Alameda 23 interacting with DEX accounts, such as SushiSwap and Uniswap. The scattered orange cluster (cluster 906) comprises Alameda accounts interacting with diverse entities, including Compound, DeFiance, and Sollet, as well as FTX, its affiliated company.

These observations highlight SBM’s capacity to identify meaningful structural divisions within the network, which aids in categorizing account behaviors and transactional patterns.

Comparison of PageRank/CheiRank centralities

Given the weak influence of inter-layer edges on the global MLTN degree distribution, a standard PageRank centrality measure may not effectively capture token transformations. To select the most suitable PageRank model, we compare their results against a baseline model (M0 PR), which applies the standard PageRank algorithm to the MLTN without inter-layer edges.

First, we compare the cumulative distribution of the centrality score with the baseline (Fig. 6), for both PageRank (left panel) and CheiRank (right panel). The Standard model aligns closely with the M0 model’s distribution, while the Biased and 2Step models exhibit deviations beyond the top 10 ranked nodes in the baseline model. The Biased model assigns higher centrality scores than the 2Step model in this range. For CheiRank, while the Standard and 2Step models closely follow the baseline, the Biased model diverges, assigning lower scores to the top 10 nodes of the baseline while increasing scores for nodes ranked between 200 and 1000.

The Spearman rank correlation analysis in Fig. 7 further supports these findings. The Biased model shows the highest deviation from the M0 model, with correlation coefficients of \(\rho = 0.75\) and 0.67 for the top 100 addresses in PageRank and CheiRank, respectively. It also exhibits the strongest discrepancy between PageRank and CheiRank rankings, with \(\rho = -0.16\) for all nodes and 0.32 for the top 100. These results indicate that the Biased model is best suited for capturing token transformations and inferring trading strategies using the PageRank-CheiRank Trade Balance (PCTB) score.

Fig. 8 and 9 present the top 10 ranked address groups and tokens across different models. For address groups, the Biased PR model exhibits the most distinct ranking, prominently featuring accounts associated with centralized and decentralized exchanges (Uniswap, FTX, and SushiSwap), which are absent in the top 10 rankings of the other models. In contrast, token rankings show minimal variation between models. While the 2Step, Standard, and Null models distribute centrality scores primarily among the top three tokens, the Biased model introduces a fourth token (WETH) with a comparable score (\(\approx 0.10\)), emphasizing its significant role in DeFi protocols.

The Standard, Biased, and 2Step PageRank models offer different perspectives on node centrality in the MLTN. The Standard model captures overall liquidity flows but tends to overemphasize highly connected nodes, such as centralized exchanges. The Biased model redistributes centrality scores to emphasize token transformations, thereby bringing DeFi entities, such as Uniswap and SushiSwap, to prominence. By setting teleportation probabilities proportional to each layer’s activity, the 2Step model emphasizes localized trading behaviors over global trends. These differences suggest that while the Standard model provides a broad overview, the Biased model is better suited for analyzing DeFi interactions, and the 2Step model helps identify short-range transactional influences.

Temporal analysis of Alameda Research’s trading behavior

Based on the MLTN network model and PageRank and CheiRank centrality metrics we proposed, we analyze monthly network snapshots to track the evolution of trading activity over time. Fig. 10 (a) presents the temporal evolution in the snapshot networks, highlighting a surge in activity for both nodes and edges before reaching stability in 2021. The burst of activities associated with inter-layer edges is slightly delayed compared to the nodes and intra-layer edges. From Q3 2022 onward, a decline begins, particularly in inter-layer edges. Fig. 10 (b) illustrates the number of newly involved addresses and tokens over time, showing a continued increase in new addresses, while the number of newly involved tokens stabilizes and then begins to decline. Fig. 10 (c) tracks transaction and edge activity for Alameda Research alongside another major fund, Jump Trading. Alameda Research’s activity peaked just before its bankruptcy, after which it ceased operations, whereas Jump Trading maintained stable activity levels. Interestingly, during 2024, we observe a temporary resurgence in Alameda Research’s activity.

Specifically, we examined Alameda Research’s trading behavior over time using the Biased PR model, comparing its trends with those of other major funds to identify patterns leading up to its bankruptcy in November 2022. Fig. 11 (a) displays the evolution of PageRank and CheiRank scores, revealing significant peaks in July 2019, October 2020, and September 2021, underscoring Alameda’s central role in the ego MLTN during these periods. In contrast, the cumulative PageRank and CheiRank scores for all other ego nodes remain lower throughout most snapshots, except after Alameda Research ceased operations in November 2022.

The PageRank-CheiRank Trade Balance (PCTB) score analysis in Fig. 11 (b) highlights reciprocal trading patterns between Alameda Research and other funds. Periods of inverse responses are observed, where positive PCTB peaks for Alameda are followed by negative peaks for other funds. These shifts occurred notably in 2019, Q1 2021, and 2022, with the strongest positive peaks coinciding with Alameda’s bankruptcy announcement. Interestingly, Q4 2020 marks a phase where both Alameda and other funds exhibit positive trade balances, indicating synchronized accumulation. By the end of the dataset (December 2024), Alameda Research enters a strong accumulation phase, indicative of token-holding behavior and a cessation of outflows.

Examining trading behavior at the token level in Fig. 11 (c), we found that Alameda Research’s activity was highly diversified, with PCTB scores varying across tokens. Table 3 provides an overview of the top tokens featured in this panel. Notably, its behavior regarding stablecoins differed: USDC exhibited a transition from a spreading phase (2020–2021) to an accumulating phase (2021–2022), whereas USDT remained relatively stable as a spreader token. ETH, heavily utilized in exchange and DeFi transactions, shows a clear shift from a spreader to an accumulator around Alameda’s bankruptcy event, reflecting a holding strategy.

Certain crypto assets experienced distinct negative PCTB events, including GRT (a governance token), SUSHI (a DEX token), and COMP (a DeFi lending and borrowing token). In the case of COMP, two strong accumulation phases are evident: one ending a year before Alameda’s collapse, followed by a shift to a spreading phase leading up to the bankruptcy. The final surge in activity within the dataset is primarily driven by ETH accumulation with sporadic stablecoin acquisitions. The only tokens for which Alameda maintains a positive balance, acting as a spreader, are WBTC and CREAM, associated with cross-chain Bitcoin trading and DeFi lending and borrowing, respectively, suggesting sustained demand for liquidity.

Our temporal analysis of Alameda Research’s trading behavior reveals distinct phases of activity, accumulation, and eventual decline. It exhibited strong market influence during peak trading periods, as indicated by its high PageRank and CheiRank scores, before transitioning to a more conservative accumulation strategy in the lead-up to its bankruptcy. The PCTB score further highlights reciprocal trading dynamics, with Alameda’s significant shifts often mirrored by opposing trends in the broader market. At the token level, its diversified activity included strategic transitions, such as the shift from spreading to accumulating ETH, alongside varying behaviors toward stablecoins and governance tokens. The final phase, marked by ETH accumulation and selective stablecoin acquisitions, suggests a shift toward long-term holding rather than active trading. These insights provides a deeper understanding of how Alameda Research has evolved its trading strategies over time and how significant market events have influenced its behavior.

Conclusion

This study introduces the Multilayer Token Network (MLTN), an extension of the Multi-Token Network framework described in Coquide et al. (2024), designed to capture the intricate inter-token dynamics essential to DeFi activities, such as token swaps and cross-token lending. By explicitly incorporating inter-layer edges, our model enhances the representation of liquidity flows across different tokens.

Focusing on an ego-network structure centered around key fund accounts (ego nodes), we assess their trading behavior using PageRank and CheiRank centralities, which serve as proxies for token accumulation and distribution, respectively. Building upon these measures, we define the PageRank-CheiRank Trade Balance (PCTB) to quantify the trading tendencies of these fund accounts.

Given the sparse nature of inter-layer edges, we introduce a Biased PageRank model that preferentially teleports across these connections, thereby improving the representation of cross-token interactions in Ethereum’s DeFi ecosystem. Our dataset spans from January 2018 to December 2024, covering critical events, including the rise and collapse of Alameda Research and FTX.

Key Findings

Our analysis of the global structure of the aggregated MLTN reveals:

  • Symmetric degree distributions between incoming and outgoing connections.

  • The presence of multiple strongly connected components (SCCs) with large diameters.

  • The network backbone depicts distinct community structures exhibiting hub-and-spoke formations, influenced by the construction of ego-networks.

Through comparisons of different PageRank models, we determine that Biased PageRank deviates the most from the baseline model, which ignores inter-layer edges, thereby maximizing PageRank-CheiRank divergence and making it the most effective choice for PCTB evaluation.

Our temporal analysis, based on monthly network snapshots, highlights bursts of network activity, characterized by an increasing number of new accounts, but a stabilizing and later declining number of traded tokens. This trend suggests a convergence toward a core set of tokens over time.

Examining Alameda Research’s trading behavior, we identify:

  • Inverse-response dynamics Alameda tended to distribute tokens when other funds were accumulating them.

  • Token-specific trading trends

    • Stablecoins USDC exhibited alternating phases of accumulation and distribution, while USDT remained predominantly in a distribution role.

    • Ethereum (ETH) A significant shift from being a distributor to becoming a dominant accumulator, particularly before Alameda’s bankruptcy.

    • DeFi tokens (e.g., WBTC, CREAM, DAI) Recent activity indicates continued ETH accumulation, accompanied by periodic distributions of WBTC, CREAM, and DAI, likely driven by liquidity requirements.

By leveraging an extended dataset, we uncover recent trading activities by Alameda Research, characterized by increased ETH accumulation and selective distribution of DeFi tokens, reflecting strategic adjustments during periods of financial distress.

Challenges and Limitations

Despite analyzing only 90 fund accounts, the MLTN rapidly expands due to the multiplicity of traded tokens and the inclusion of ego-network partners (alters), resulting in a large yet sparse structure. Addressing this computational challenge could involve:

  • Reducing the number of token layers while preserving critical trading patterns to enhance computational efficiency.

  • Expanding beyond ego-networks by incorporating alters as additional ego nodes, reducing sparsity, and providing a more comprehensive view of DeFi interactions.

  • Hybridizing the Biased and 2Step PageRank models to better balance inter-layer influence and token flow dynamics.

Future work

Future research could focus on exploring correlations between fund trading behaviors and DeFi smart contracts, potentially revealing hidden partnerships and systemic risks. Additionally, adopting an improved temporal resolution-such as a stream graph formalism based on block-time transfers-could provide deeper insights into the real-time evolution of trading dynamics. Refining these approaches will enhance behavioral pattern detection, improve risk assessment models, and strengthen systemic risk analysis in the DeFi ecosystem.

Data availability

The dataset used in this study consists of Ethereum blockchain transaction data from January 2018 to December 2024. Due to the on-chain nature of the data, it is publicly available and can be accessed via blockchain explorers (e.g., Etherscan) or through blockchain data providers (e.g., Alchemy). Any additional processed data supporting this study’s findings are available from the corresponding author upon request.
